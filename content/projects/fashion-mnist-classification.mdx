## Problem

Fashion-MNIST is a standard benchmark of small grayscale photos of clothing:
28 by 28 pixels, ten classes such as T-shirt, trouser, coat, sandal and bag,
and a test set of 10,000 images. Some classes look much alike at that size.
A shirt, a T-shirt and a coat differ mostly in the neckline and sleeves.

This was an individual project for an MS machine learning course. The
question was how far each family of model gets on the same data, from a
classical support vector machine to a convolutional network, and what it
takes to close the gap.

## Approach

I built every model in one codebase, with a configuration file per
experiment so each run could be repeated and compared.

- **Classical baseline.** I reduced each image to its main components with
  principal component analysis (PCA) and trained a support vector machine
  (SVM) with a radial basis function kernel on them, testing how many
  components to keep. I also trained a plain fully connected network on the
  pixels.
- **Convolutional network.** The SVM and the fully connected network both
  see an image as one long row of pixels, which throws away which pixels are
  next to each other. A convolutional neural network (CNN) keeps the 2D
  layout and learns local shapes such as edges and curves. My baseline CNN
  used the Adam optimizer at a fixed learning rate of 0.002, with no dropout
  and no schedule.
- **Tuned CNN.** I then added a third convolutional layer, dropout of 0.3,
  the AdamW optimizer with weight decay, and a cosine schedule that lowers
  the learning rate smoothly over training.
- **A sanity check.** Finally I collapsed the ten classes into two,
  footwear (sandal, sneaker, ankle boot) and everything else, to see how
  well the CNN captures overall shape.

## Results & Impact

Test accuracy on the 10,000 test images:

- SVM on PCA features, best at 100 components: 86.62%
- SVM and standard fully connected network, best runs: both topped out near 88%
- Baseline CNN: 90.87%
- Deeper CNN with dropout, AdamW and a cosine schedule: 93.39%

<Figure src="/images/projects/fashion-mnist-classification/baseline-cnn-loss.webp" alt="Line chart titled Training and Validation Loss for the baseline CNN, loss against epoch. Train loss in blue falls steadily. Validation loss in red jumps up early, then falls, then rises again over the last epochs." caption="Training loss (blue) and validation loss (red) of my baseline CNN. The validation loss jumps early in training and drifts back up at the end." width="1033" height="672" />

The baseline CNN beat the SVM on PCA features by more than 4 points and
reached a weighted F1 score of 0.91, a sign that keeping the image's spatial
structure matters on this data. The tuned network raised accuracy to 93.39%
with a weighted F1 of 0.93, and its loss curve was smoother and steadier than
the baseline's.

<Figure src="/images/projects/fashion-mnist-classification/tuned-cnn-loss.webp" alt="Line chart titled Training and Validation Loss for the tuned CNN, loss against epoch. Train loss in blue and validation loss in red both fall and end lower than the baseline's, with the validation loss levelling off instead of rising at the end." caption="The same curves for the tuned CNN with dropout, AdamW and the cosine schedule. Both losses end lower than the baseline's (the scales differ), and the validation loss levels off instead of climbing at the end." width="1027" height="672" />

The confusion matrices showed where the remaining errors are. Shirt was the
hardest class, most often mistaken for T-shirt or coat. The
tuned CNN improved it but did not solve it. On the footwear task the CNN made
only 14 mistakes in 10,000 test images. Telling a shoe from a piece of
clothing is easy for it. The hard part is telling similar tops apart.
