## The paper

Steganography hides a message so that nobody notices there is one. The
classic way to hide an image inside another is to overwrite the least
significant bits of each pixel of the cover image with the most significant
bits of the secret image. The result looks normal to the eye, but it leaves
traces that steganalysis tools can detect.

The paper we reproduced, from NeurIPS 2017, hides a full size color image
inside a cover image of the same size using three convolutional neural
networks trained together. A preparation network turns the secret image into
useful features, a hiding network blends them into the cover image to make a
container image, and a reveal network recovers the secret from the container.
Because the networks are trained as a pair, they decide for themselves where
and how to spread the secret across the bits of the cover, instead of
following a fixed rule. The paper trained on images from ImageNet.

The paper also shows its own weak spot. If you take the difference between
the cover and the container and enhance it strongly (the paper uses 20
times), parts of the secret image become visible. And the method makes no
explicit attempt to hide from machine detection that a secret is there at
all.

## What we reproduced

We were a team of three in an MS course. We rebuilt the three network model
and trained it on Tiny ImageNet, since full ImageNet needed more compute than
we had: 1,250 pairs of cover and secret images at 64 by 64 pixels, with 1,000
pairs for training and 250 for testing.

We then extended the paper in two directions.

- **Encryption of the hidden image.** Before hiding the secret image, we
  scrambled the positions of its pixels with a permutation. Without the
  permutation, a revealed image is just scrambled pixels. The permutation
  list is large, so we encrypted it with AES, a fast symmetric cipher, and
  encrypted the AES key itself with RSA, a public key cipher. The receiver
  uses the RSA private key to recover the AES key, decrypts the permutation
  with it, and unscrambles the revealed image.
- **Training changes.** The paper trains with a mean squared error loss on
  the pixels. We added a term based on SSIM, the structural similarity index,
  which scores how alike two images look in structure rather than pixel by
  pixel. We also compared tanh and ReLU activations, and trained the updated
  model for 250 epochs.

## What we found

ReLU activations worked better than tanh, and adding the SSIM term improved
training and the quality of the results.

To see how much of the secret leaks into the container, we compared the
residual between cover and container at 1, 3 and 5 times enhancement against
the reference paper's results. Our model left less visible trace of the
secret image at all three levels. The comparison has a caveat: we trained on
small 64 by 64 images, not on the paper's ImageNet images.

The encryption has room to grow. Our permutation was generated from a fixed
seed, and a stronger scheme would add more security. We also noted that
newer generative models, such as GANs and diffusion models, could produce
cleaner container images than an encoder and decoder network.
