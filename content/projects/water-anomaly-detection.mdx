## Problem

Water treatment plants are cyber-physical systems. Industrial controllers
drive pumps, valves and dosing units from dozens of sensor readings, and an
attacker who reaches that network can push a plant into an unsafe state.
Attacks rarely show up in one sensor. If someone spoofs the level reading of
a tank, the pump overfills it, and minutes later pressure and flow readings
further down the plant start to drift. No single reading looks wrong. The
attack is visible only as a pattern across many sensors over time.

My MS thesis, "Graph-Based and Ensemble Anomaly Detection for Smart Water
Treatment Systems", asked how to catch those attacks from the sensor streams
alone, reliably enough that an operator would trust the alerts, and with an
explanation the operator can act on. I did the work in the Cybersecurity Lab
at the University of Arkansas, advised by Dr. Qinghua Li, in collaboration
with Dr. Feras Batarseh of the A3 Lab at Virginia Tech and Dr. MD Nazmul
Kabir Sikder of Old Dominion University.

My main testbed was SWaT, a real water treatment testbed with 51 sensors and
actuators spread across six process stages. Its 2015 data contains 35
labeled attack segments.

## Approach

I started from the Graph Deviation Network (GDN), published at AAAI 2021.
It gives every sensor a learned embedding, links each sensor to the few
others it most resembles, predicts each sensor's next reading from its
neighbors, and raises an alarm when a prediction misses. It learns only from
normal operation. Around it I built five pieces.

- **Training changes.** Some random seeds produced a failed run, so I added
  orthogonal embedding initialization, which gives each sensor a distinct
  starting point, along with gradient clipping and a learning rate warmup.
- **A reconstruction decoder.** Some attacks, such as a slow ramp, a fixed
  offset or a sensor stuck at one value, are easy to predict, so the
  prediction error stays low and the model lets them through. Once GDN was
  trained, I froze it and trained a small second network, the decoder, to
  rebuild the recent input window from GDN's internal features. Each sensor
  then has a prediction error and a reconstruction error, and the larger one
  counts.
- **A learnable decision head.** A small neural network that reads the per
  sensor scores and decides when to raise an alarm, in place of a single
  fixed threshold.
- **An ensemble.** Several GDN models that differ in random seed, number of
  neighbors and learning rate, with their per sensor scores averaged into
  one score.
- **Explaining each alert.** For every attack I ranked the sensors by score,
  mapped the top ones to the plant's six physical stages, and ran a two hop
  breadth first search on the learned graph to trace how the attack spread.
  A structured summary of that analysis goes to the Claude API, which writes
  a plain language alert for the operator, for example naming the valve in
  the ultrafiltration stage to check. An interactive dashboard shows the
  results.

## Architecture & Tech

The pipeline is Python and PyTorch, and every run is driven by a
configuration file and saved with its settings, so each result can be traced
back to the run that produced it. It has six stages:

1. The GDN backbone, trained on normal data only.
2. The frozen reconstruction decoder, trained after the backbone.
3. Scoring: the prediction and reconstruction errors of each sensor are
   normalized, smoothed over the last few steps and fused by taking the
   larger, and the highest sensor score is the score for that moment.
4. The decision head, which turns scores into alerts.
5. The ensemble, which averages the per sensor scores of its members.
6. Root cause localization on the learned graph, the Claude API for the
   operator alert, and the dashboard.

Besides SWaT I used WADI, a water distribution testbed with 127 sensors.

## Results & Impact

My thesis reported higher numbers than the ones on this page. In November
2026 I audited how they had been produced. Training was clean: no test data
and no test labels reached it. The steps after training were not clean: scores
were normalized with statistics of the test set, thresholds were chosen with
the test labels, ensemble members were picked by their test score, and the
decision head was trained on labeled test data. So every number I had
reported depended on test labels or test statistics. I rebuilt the
evaluation and ran the work again, and the figures below come from that.

How the results are measured now:

- **Data.** The attack free training file is cut by time into three parts:
  the first 80% trains the model, the next 10% picks the checkpoint and
  trains the decoder, and the last 10% is calibration data, held back to set
  the normalization and a label free threshold, which is simply the largest
  score seen on calibration data, so it needs no labels. The test file is
  used only to compute the final metrics and, where a result says so, the
  normalization statistics. Seeds 0 to 19 were fixed before any run, and
  single model results are a mean and standard deviation over those 20
  seeds. Figures for one member of the ensemble pool below are a mean and
  standard deviation over the 45 models in the pool.
- **Oracle F1.** F1 combines how many attack moments are caught with how
  many alarms are real. Oracle F1 is the best F1 over every possible
  threshold. Finding that threshold uses the test labels, which no plant has
  while it runs, so Oracle F1 is an upper bound on what the detector can do,
  not a deployable result.
- **Normalization statistics.** Before errors from different sensors can be
  compared, each one has that sensor's median error subtracted and is then
  divided by the spread of its errors. Where those statistics come from
  turned out to matter a lot, so I report three sources: the calibration
  data; past observations only, meaning the calibration data plus the test readings before the
  moment being scored; and the whole test set. The last one is the
  convention of the original GDN code and of the papers that reuse it. It
  means the normalization has seen the entire test period, including
  readings after the moment it scores.

What holds on SWaT under that protocol:

- **Single model.** GDN with my training changes, scoring all 51 sensors
  with test statistics, reaches an Oracle F1 of 0.709 ± 0.101 over 20 seeds,
  and 4 of 20 runs fail with an Oracle F1 near 0.52. The training changes
  make no measurable difference: with the original setup, 4 of 20 runs fail
  as well. Other training settings, including those in the GDN paper, still
  produced failed runs when all 51 sensors are scored. The false alarms in
  failed runs come largely from pumps that never change state in the
  training data but do switch during normal operation in the test period.
- **Leaving out constant sensors.** 14 of the 51 sensors never change in the
  training data. Leaving them out of the score is a rule that needs no
  labels, and it removes the failures: 0.760 ± 0.014 over 20 seeds with test
  statistics, with no run below 0.60.
- **Normalization.** On those 37 sensors, the same 20 models score 0.760
  with test statistics, 0.593 ± 0.139 with past observations only, and
  0.431 ± 0.214 with calibration statistics. That shift is larger than
  anything I changed in the model.
- **Reconstruction decoder.** Trained for three epochs, as in the thesis,
  the decoder had barely learned anything and lowered Oracle F1 from 0.709
  to 0.630 (all 51 sensors, 20 seeds). Trained until its loss stops falling,
  it gives 0.77 to 0.78 ± 0.02 with no failed run (all 51 sensors, test
  statistics). On the 37 sensors the gain is +0.007, within noise, and with
  past or calibration statistics the decoder slightly lowers the score. Its
  clearer benefit is the label free threshold: on the 37
  sensors with test statistics, F1 at that threshold is 0.752 ± 0.051 with
  the decoder and 0.418 ± 0.375 without it.
- **Ensemble.** I trained a pool of 45 models. On the 37 sensors with test
  statistics, averaging all 45 gives an Oracle F1 of 0.779, against
  0.766 ± 0.016 for one member of the pool, a modest gain. The clearer
  effect is that models miss different attacks: at the Oracle threshold the
  ensemble of all 45 detects 18 of 35 attack segments, against 11.0 for a
  single member on average. With past observations only, the ensemble does not
  help (0.651 for all 45, against 0.658 ± 0.153 for one member). Choosing
  members by their test score, as my thesis code did, adds about 0.02 to
  0.03 that is selection rather than detection.
- **Decision head.** The thesis result for it is withdrawn. It was trained
  on labeled test timesteps split at random into 30% and 70%, and
  neighboring timesteps, which are nearly identical, landed on both sides
  of the split, so the held out part was not an independent test.
- **The old numbers.** The thesis's headline figures came from models
  chosen by their test score. Repeating that selection on models trained
  with the new code gives figures close to the thesis's, so the gap comes
  from the selection, not from the models. The old figures are withdrawn.
- **Root cause and alerts.** The audit did not rerun root cause
  localization or the Claude API alerts. Both are built as described above, but
  their thesis figures came from the earlier evaluation, so I leave them out
  until they are measured under the new protocol.

The main lesson is that how a detector is evaluated, where the
normalization statistics and the threshold come from, changed the picture
more than any change I made to the model. A fuller comparison is in a paper
my coauthors and I are preparing.

## What I'd do next

The open problem is the drop from 0.760 to 0.593 when the score may not look
at future test data: I want a score that holds up with past observations or
calibration data alone. I would also measure root cause localization and the
operator alerts under the new protocol, let the sensor graph update as plant
conditions change, connect the pipeline to a plant's live data historian,
and test the generated alerts with real operators.
