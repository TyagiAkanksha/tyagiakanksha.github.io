## Problem

When an ambulance is stuck in traffic, every second of delay can matter.
Route planners decide which roads an emergency vehicle takes, but they
mostly ignore the lane it drives in, and on a busy road with several lanes,
being in the right lane at the right moment saves real time. Fixed rules for
lane changes handle this poorly. At IIIT Hyderabad, advised by Dr. Praveen
Paruchuri, we set out to learn the lane choice instead.

## Approach

I framed lane selection for the emergency vehicle as a Markov decision
process and trained a reinforcement learning policy on it, which we called
Reinforcement Learning based Lane Search.

- **State.** What the emergency vehicle knows about the vehicle directly
  ahead of it (the distance between them, their speed difference and that
  vehicle's acceleration), plus the free space in each lane within
  vehicle to vehicle (V2V) communication range.
- **Action.** Which lane to be in, decided again at every interval.
- **Reward.** At each interval, the distance the vehicle covered is compared
  with the distance it would have covered on an empty road. The gap is
  squared so that large losses count more, clipped to keep learning stable,
  and summed over the trip for an episode score.
- **Algorithm.** A2C, trained for 700,000 steps in Python and PyTorch.

I trained three variants: one with only the per interval reward, one that
added the episode reward, and one that also trained in congested traffic.
The second, trained on normal traffic with both rewards, did best in every
setting, including congested traffic it had never trained on.

## Architecture & Tech

The environment is SUMO, the open source traffic simulator, driven from
Python through its TraCI interface. The road is a 2 km one way stretch with
four lanes. In normal traffic a new vehicle enters with a 60% chance every
second; congestion is simulated by slowing several vehicles at regular
intervals, for example three every 40 seconds or nine every 60 seconds.

I compared the learned policy with three heuristic baselines: SUMO's default
lane changing, a policy that stays in one fixed lane, and a best lane
heuristic that weighs the utility of each lane. A run on an empty road gives
the lower bound on traversal time.

To check the policy against real traffic, I used a week of New York City
traffic speed data from the city's arterial roads, 9,670 minutes in all. I
grouped road speeds into bands 5 m/s wide, made 100 runs per setting, one
setting for each band, and weighted the bands by how often they occur in the
data.

## Results & Impact

- **Simulated congestion.** Traversal time fell by up to 10.9% against the
  best lane heuristic, the strongest of the three baselines.
- **Simulated normal traffic.** Traversal time fell by 6% against the same
  heuristic.
- **New York City data.** The policy beat the best lane heuristic by 2.5% in
  normal traffic and 4% in congested traffic.

I wrote the response to the peer reviewers defending the method, and the
paper, "Improving Lane Level Dynamics for EV Traversal: A Reinforcement
Learning Approach", was accepted as a full paper at VEHITS 2024, where 21% of
full paper submissions were accepted. I am its first author, with Meghna
Lowalekar and Dr. Praveen Paruchuri. It was a Best Student Paper nominee, and
I presented it online at the conference in May 2024.

## What I'd do next

The policy has been tested on straight roads. I would extend it to
intersections, combine lane choice with route planning so the two decisions
are made together, and test it when only some vehicles share data over V2V
and their readings are noisy.
