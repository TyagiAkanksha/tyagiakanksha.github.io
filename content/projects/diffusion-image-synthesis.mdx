## The paper

A diffusion model learns to make images by learning to undo noise. During
training, an image is buried step by step under Gaussian noise, and a U-Net
learns to predict and remove a little of that noise at each step. To generate,
the model starts from pure noise and denoises it into a picture. Doing this on
raw pixels is expensive: the paper notes that training the strongest pixel
space models can take hundreds of GPU days.

The latent diffusion paper splits the job in two. An autoencoder first learns
to compress an image into a much smaller latent representation and to decode
it back. Diffusion then runs in that small latent space, so the U-Net spends
its effort on what the image shows rather than on every pixel. For text to
image generation, a text encoder turns the prompt into embeddings, and the
U-Net reads them through cross attention at each denoising step. The paper's
own conclusion, which we restated in our presentation, is that this makes both
training and sampling more efficient while keeping image quality.

## What we did

This was a two person project for an MS course. Rather
than train a model from scratch, we took a pretrained latent diffusion
checkpoint and fine tuned it on a new domain: faces described in words. We
used CelebA-Dialog, a dataset of over 200,000 celebrity photos, each with
captions describing the face, such as how broadly the person is smiling. We
wrote a data loader that feeds each image to the model together with its
caption.

The model kept the paper's structure. A pretrained KL autoencoder that
downsamples by 8 turns each 256 by 256 image into a 32 by 32 latent with 4
channels. The U-Net denoises in that latent space and is conditioned on
captions from two frozen text encoders, BERT and the CLIP ViT-L/14 text model.

## My part

- I ran the fine tuning: 256 pixel images at batch size 32 on a single A100
  GPU, the largest batch that fit in its memory.
- I found and fixed the run that was not learning. With a learning rate of
  5e-5, the loss stayed flat for 65 epochs, about three to four days of
  continuous training. After more experiments I dropped the rate to 2e-7,
  with a 100 step warmup followed by a cosine schedule.
- I presented the method and our results at the course's final
  presentation.

## What we found

The lower learning rate is what made the fine tuning work. The loss fell to
about a tenth of what the first run had reached, with less training. Samples
from the first run were still mostly noise, while by epoch 37 the tuned model
produced faces that followed their captions, for prompts such as "a young
woman with broad smile" and "a man with no smile".

The lesson we took away is about fine tuning in general. The learning rate
the authors used for their own models did not suit fine tuning on our
dataset. Adapting the pretrained model to captioned faces called for a
learning rate 250 times smaller than our first run's 5e-5, and with that rate
fine tuning gave good results even with fewer epochs.
