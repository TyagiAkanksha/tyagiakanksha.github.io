## The paper

A PET scan measures where a radioactive tracer collects in the body, and
software has to reconstruct a 3D image from the raw detector data. With a
low dose of tracer or a short scan, that data is noisy and so is the image.
The standard iterative method, OSEM, uses no prior knowledge about the
image. MAPEM adds a hand designed prior, here guided by an MR scan of
the same patient, which suppresses noise but tends to blur real detail,
including small lesions.

Deep learning offers other routes. A network can clean up an image after a
standard reconstruction, as a U-Net denoiser does, or learn the whole
mapping from raw data to image, which takes a great deal of training data.
The paper we reproduced, "Model-Based Deep Learning PET Image Reconstruction
Using Forward-Backward Splitting Expectation Maximisation", takes a middle
path. Its method, FBSEM, keeps the physics of the scanner and learns only
the prior. Each iteration has three steps: a small convolutional network
denoises the current image, an expectation maximisation (EM) update pulls it
back toward what the scanner actually measured, and a fusion step weighs the
two. The iterations are unrolled into a recurrent network that reuses the
same parameters at every step, so the model stays small.

The results in this section are the paper's, not ours. The authors report
that FBSEM performs as well as U-Net denoising with fewer parameters. On
simulated test data, the PET only variant of FBSEM underestimated hot lesion intensity
by 7.4% on average, against 25.7% for MAPEM, which tends to smooth lesions
away.

## What we reproduced

We were a team of three in an MS course, and we implemented and trained
FBSEM.

- **Data.** We used BrainWeb, a widely used set of simulated brain scans:
  70 PET and MR brain phantoms with hot and cold lesions added at random,
  with low dose and high dose scans simulated for each, including noise and
  the scanner's blur. Each PET volume is 344 by 344 by 128 voxels. We
  trained on 200 datasets and tested on 10.
- **Preparation.** We trimmed the raw sinograms radially to cut computation,
  and started each reconstruction from 10 iterations of OSEM.
- **Model.** The learned prior is a 5 layer convolutional network with 32
  filters per layer, batch normalization, ReLU activations and a residual
  connection. With the EM update and the fusion step, and all of it shared
  across iterations, the whole network has about 28,000 parameters. We
  trained the variant that takes both PET and MR as input.
- **Training.** We minimized the mean squared error against the high dose
  images with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 0.05, one 3D volume
  per batch. Training from scratch for 50 epochs took about 36 hours.
- **Evaluation.** We measured contrast to noise ratio between gray and white
  matter, normalized root mean squared error against the high dose image,
  and the error in hot lesion intensity.

## What we found

The 7.4% and 25.7% error figures above are the paper's, and our run did not
reproduce that comparison.

Training converged gradually but stably. Comparing reconstructions at
epochs 4, 17, 38 and 50, FBSEM's images grew sharper, with clearer
boundaries between gray and white matter, and by the last epoch they were
close to the high dose reference. The MAPEM reconstruction of the same data
stayed somewhat oversmoothed. Because the network shares its parameters
across iterations, it can also run more iterations at test time without
retraining.

We did not get as far as the paper's main comparison. We trained only the
PET and MR variant, so our next steps were to train the PET only
FBSEM and the U-Net baselines under the same settings and data. Until then,
the finding that FBSEM keeps up with U-Net using fewer parameters rests on
the paper, not on our run.
